Pondr

Fifteen minutes a day, on a subject you chose.

There is a subject that has been going round your head for months: a work idea, a family matter, a decision you cannot make. Pondr gives it a fifteen-minute appointment a day. You choose the subject. The app asks one concrete question and keeps it on the screen while the phone lies face down. Then you write a page. Then the mirror does what a good friend does: reads what you wrote from outside, in your name and in the third person, and asks exactly one question. Tomorrow you sit with that question.

One day

Four moves, every day

A question you can picture

Not “how do you feel about your father” but “the first scene that comes up at the word father: where are you, who is there, what is said?” A scene, not the name of a feeling.

Fifteen minutes, phone face down

The question stays on the screen; you are not meant to look at it. Under fifteen minutes the measured effect of this kind of writing disappears, so the clock is not a setting.

A page, in your own words

Writing is the one step with randomised-trial evidence behind it. Five words are enough on a bad day; “nothing to say” is an answer the app accepts without comment.

The mirror

A short note about what it noticed, in the third person and by your name, and one question that puts you outside your own situation. No advice, no praise, no verdict. It is not a chat: its turn is tomorrow.

Day four

Your own sentences, side by side

Four sittings on one subject make an arc. At the end the app puts what you wrote on the first day next to what you wrote on the fourth. No score, no graph. The distance between two sentences is yours; nobody can hand it to you, because you wrote both.

Day 1 · 22 SepDeniz stood in front of the shop for an hour and did not go in; she wrote that she had no courage.
Day 4 · 25 SepDeniz went in, talked to the owner, and told her mother “I’m not deciding, I’m looking.” The fear did not leave, but she was inside.

Self-distancing Kross

Speaking of yourself by name, in the third person, calms the feeling and sharpens the thinking. That is why the mirror calls you by name and never says “you”.

The visible question Westgate

A question in front of your eyes works better than thinking at random; people underestimate how rewarding thinking is when a prompt stays in view. That is why it stays on the screen for fifteen minutes.

Expressive writing Pennebaker · Frattaroli

Putting what you lived into words works, most of all with regular repetition; under fifteen minutes and under three sittings the measured effect disappears. That is why every sitting ends in writing, and why a subject takes four days.

When, then what Gollwitzer

An intention tied to a moment is kept far more often than one kept in the head (d = .65). That is why subjects close with a plan you write yourself: when, then what.

A subject you chose Ryan & Deci · Nguyen

Solitude works when the subject is your own, not assigned. The app never hands you a topic; it asks what brought you here and lets you pick.

The Life modules values · direction · the small safe step · letting go · the Decision Room · the life audit · the letter · Misogi · the manifesto · looking back

Each is the same fifteen minutes with different questions: four days on your values in your own words; a year-from-now morning as a scene and the first step; a ten-minute safe step toward what you avoid, which you write and the app never proposes; a habit or a decision from every side; a monthly look from above; a sealed letter that opens in a year; one hard thing a year; one page on how you want to live; and your own sentences read back a month and a year later.

What you write stays on your phone. There is no account, because we do not want your data.

To prepare the mirror, that day’s entry, your first name and the day’s question go once through our server to OpenAI’s model and come back as a note and a question; nothing is stored on the way, and entry text is never logged. The app asks your explicit permission before the first send, and “don’t send, just save” is a real option. Entries that show signs of crisis never leave the phone at all; a human-written support screen appears instead.

A look inside

Five screens

Why fifteen minutes, why four days, why the third person.

Fifteen minutes because in the expressive-writing research the effect disappears below that dose. Four sittings because the benefit does not show before the third. The third person because looking at yourself from outside lowers the heat and sharpens the judgement (Kross). A question kept on the screen because people think better with one in front of them (Westgate). A subject you chose because assigned solitude does not work (Ryan & Deci). What we sell is not psychological change; it is the felt experience of having actually thought about something, which is the best-evidenced effect there is.
